# Chapter 1

## A Revelation of Jesus Christ from God the Father

### Revelation 1:1–3

> 1 The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things that must soon take place. He made it known by sending his angel to his servant John, 2 who bore witness to the word of God and to the testimony of Jesus Christ, even to all that he saw. 3 Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear, and who keep what is written in it, for the time is near.

The opening verse of this book invites us to slow down and listen closely. This revelation of Jesus Christ comes directly from God the Father. That truth alone tells us that we are standing on holy ground. We are reading and hearing a disclosure about God himself in the person of his Son, given to us by the Father. "*The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him*".

Imagine what receiving this message must have been like for the apostle John. At the time of this vision, he was living in exile on the island of Patmos. He was after all, "*the disciple whom Jesus loved*" (John 13:23). He had shared meals with Jesus before the crucifixion and had fellowshipped with Him after the resurrection. Yet, only a few verses later in this first chapter, when John sees his risen and glorified King, he falls at his feet "as though dead" (Revelation 1:17). We are truly dealing with holy mysteries here that are to be greatly treasured. Be sure to take time to pray and worship God as you read the mysteries of this book!

Two difficult questions rise almost immediately in this prologue: *What does John mean when he says that these things "must soon take place" (v. 1), and what does he mean when he concludes that "the time is near" (v. 3)?* We are living in the twenty-first century, and while some of Revelation’s themes were already unfolding in John’s day, much of what the book describes has plainly not yet happened. We should not avoid these questions; they are the elephant in the room for many readers. Yet, Scripture gives us good reasons not to be troubled by the timeline that "soon" and "near" may seem to imply. Let's look at why this is the case and gain some clarity on what John very likely means.

The phrase translated "soon" or "shortly" in most English Bibles is *en tachei*. This underlying Greek root can carry two meanings: chronological imminence ("soon" or "without delay") or qualitative velocity ("quickly" or "with swiftness"). The New Testament uses this phrase in both ways. In Acts 12:7, an angel commands the sleeping Peter to rise "quickly"; conversely, in Romans 16:20 and 1 Timothy 3:14, it is rendered "soon" or "shortly" to describe an impending timeframe.[^1]

While it is popular to think this phrase only refers to a calendar countdown, a significant lineage of classical Greek scholars, commentators, and early church history readers held the belief that the text signifies a sudden and explosive velocity of execution once the prophetic timeline is initiated. It emphasizes the swiftness of events *once God begins them*—a rapid execution after the start takes place.

For example, nineteenth-century classical scholars like E.W. Bullinger and Walter Scott argued that *en tachei* describes the sudden, unhindered *manner* of fulfillment rather than a localized first-century calendar expectation. The renowned New Testament grammarian A.T. Robertson similarly noted that words sharing this root emphasize swiftness of motion, highlighting the etymological link to velocity rather than a simple delay factor. Modern expositors such as John MacArthur and David Guzik have carried this forward, demonstrating that when these end-time judgments are revealed in history, they will unfold in rapid, uninterrupted succession like birth pains.[^2]

Just as John uses *en tachei* to describe the *manner* of these events, he uses the Greek word ***engys*** (**ἐγγύς**) at the end of verse 3 to state that the time is "near". In the New Testament, *engys* can signify physical proximity (being close by in space) or temporal proximity (being close by in time). In the context of prophecy, however, *engys* functions as a spiritual reality rather than a strict calendar countdown. It signals that the next major epoch on God's redemptive calendar has arrived and stands right at the threshold.[^3]

This combined language of chronological proximity (*engys*) and rapid execution (*en tachei*) has been intentionally designed by the Holy Spirit to keep every generation of the Church in a state of wakeful expectancy. As John Piper notes,[^4] it forces us to live with the constant "*reality that Christ is always at the gates*".

> Therefore, preparing your minds for action, and being sober-minded, set your hope fully on the grace that will be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ. (1 Peter 1:13 ESV)

> Stay dressed for action and keep your lamps burning, and be like men who are waiting for their master to come home from the wedding feast, so that they may open the door to him at once when he comes and knocks. Blessed are those servants whom the master finds awake when he comes. Truly, I say to you, he will dress himself for service and have them recline at table, and he will come and serve them. If he comes in the second watch, or in the third, and finds them awake, blessed are those servants! But know this, that if the master of the house had known at what hour the thief was coming, he would not have left his house to be broken into. You also must be ready, for the Son of Man is coming at an hour you do not expect. (Luke 12:35-40 ESV)

This linguistic clarity is essential to understanding what "*soon*" and "*near*" mean. It untangles the book of Revelation as a whole from theories that incorrectly insist all these events had to be entirely fulfilled in the ancient past, such as around A.D. 70 (*see Preterism in Appendix A: Definitions*). At the same time, it avoids the need to construct highly complicated, multi-stage escape timelines that are nowhere explicitly stated in the text. We do not need to rest the whole matter on a single Greek phrase, nor do we need to look for a secret, hidden rescue of the Church before the final trial.[^5] Instead, we simply look at how Scripture itself constructs its prophetic timeline.

Moreover, a structural parallel to Revelation 1:1 is found in the Greek Septuagint translation of Daniel 2:28–29, where things that "must take place" (*ha dei genesthai*) are unveiled.[^6]

> But there is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries, and he has made known to King Nebuchadnezzar *what must take place* in the latter days (Dan. 2:28 LXX).

The significance of this parallel is that the opening words of Revelation are a deliberate echo of Daniel 2 in the Greek Old Testament. By using the exact phrase *ha dei genesthai*, John is signaling to his readers that the mysterious end-time prophecies given to the prophet Daniel are finally being unlocked and fulfilled in the Revelation of Jesus.

Furthermore, the Greek word *dei* ("it is necessary" or "must") often expresses divine necessity. It expresses that events will unfold according to God’s revealed purpose rather than by human chance. In Daniel’s vision, God unveils what "*must take place*" in the latter days (Dan. 2:28–29, 45 LXX). Jesus says that the Messiah "*must*" suffer and enter His glory in fulfillment of Scripture (Luke 9:22; 24:26, 44–46). Revelation uses the same language for events that "*must* soon take place" (Rev. 1:1; 22:6).

This sense of fulfillment also aligns consistently with the testimony of the rest of the New Testament. At Pentecost, Peter argued that the outpouring of the Holy Spirit fulfilled God’s promise for "the last days" (Acts 2:17). Similarly, the author of Hebrews notes that God has spoken to us by his Son "in these last days" (Hebrews 1:2). John himself wrote in his first letter that because opponents of Christ had already arrived, "it is the last hour" (1 John 2:18). From Pentecost onward, the apostles understood themselves to be living in the final era of human history. And this should be our understanding today.

Another example that helps explain Revelation’s prophetic timeline is Jesus’ parable of the persistent widow. Jesus told this parable so that his disciples would "always pray and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1). He concludes with these words:

> And the Lord said, "Hear what the unrighteous judge says. And will not God give justice to his elect, who cry to him day and night? Will he delay long over them? I tell you, he will give justice to them speedily. Nevertheless, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?" (Luke 18:6–8 ESV)

The widow waits. From a human perspective, the answer seems slow in coming. Yet Jesus asks, "*Will he delay long over them?*" and promises that God will give justice "*speedily*," using the same phrase, *en tachei*, found in Revelation 1:1. Both realities are true: God’s people may endure a season of waiting, but God is neither indifferent nor hesitant.

Notice, too, where Jesus takes the parable: to the coming of the Son of Man and to the question of whether he will find faith on the earth. The promise of swift vindication stands beside a pastoral call to persistent prayer and persevering faith. Jesus’ question warns believers not to lose heart during the period of waiting, whatever pressures they may face.

Paul speaks in the same way to the believers in Rome: "The God of peace will *soon* (*en tachei*) crush Satan under your feet" (Romans 16:20). Satan’s decisive defeat was won at the cross, though his final destruction awaits Christ’s return (Revelation 20:10). Even so, Paul uses the word "*soon*." The prophet Habakkuk likewise says, "If it seems slow, wait for it" (Habakkuk 2:3), and the writer of Hebrews applies that promise directly to Christians: "*the coming one will come and will not delay*" (Hebrews 10:37). Therefore, the use of "*soon*" and "*near*" in Revelation should be understood to mean how decisively God will act when he orchestrates these final events.

Consequently, the words "*soon*" and "*near*" in the beginning verses of John's letter carry a dual purpose. First, they signify that the drama of the final era has already been set in motion, meaning that the kingdom of God has drawn close enough to break into human history. Second, they imply urgency: as the events recorded in Revelation unfold in their respective eras, they will be carried out swiftly and without delay. Therefore, Christians are called to anticipate the return of Jesus as a reality that is always imminent and are to live in a manner that reflects this.

This perspective explains why the letters to the historical churches in chapters 2 and 3 were preserved for us and why they still matter practically today. The dark pressures of persecution, spiritual compromise, emperor worship, and the danger of growing lukewarm were intensely real for those early followers of Christ. They were being called to remain faithful, endure great suffering, and face death if required. While these extreme trials may seem distant to many readers in the West, they represent a daily, dangerous reality for Christians in other parts of the world today.

At the same time, the contemporary Western church faces its own subtle versions of these ancient struggles, particularly regarding where believers place their deepest allegiance. In the United States, for instance, many Christians tie their identity deeply to political parties or leaders, such as the Republican Party and Donald Trump. This reality raises a fundamental question that Revelation was written to address: *Where does a Christian's ultimate allegiance, worship, and trust belong?* Scripture's answer is clear: it belongs exclusively to God.

The Old Testament prophets speak in this same dual way. Isaiah, Ezekiel, Joel, and Zephaniah all declared that the day of the Lord was "near" (*engys* in the Septuagint) (Isaiah 13:6; Ezekiel 30:3; Joel 1:15; Zephaniah 1:7). Zephaniah even called it "near and hastening fast" (Zephaniah 1:14). Often those warnings were fulfilled in localized judgments that fell in the prophets’ own lifetimes, yet the same language points ahead to the final cosmic day of the Lord. This is not failed prophecy. It is simply how God speaks about his judgment: it is near, it is certain, and it presses upon the human conscience. It calls us to live watchfully today, not to calculate dates for tomorrow.

Paul takes up this exact theme in his first letter to the Thessalonians:

> Now concerning the times and the seasons, brothers, you have no need to have anything written to you. 2 *For you yourselves are fully aware that the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night.* While people are saying, "There is peace and security," *then sudden destruction will come* upon them as labor pains come upon a pregnant woman, and they will not escape. *But you are not in darkness, brothers, for that day to surprise you like a thief.* For you are all children of light, children of the day. We are not of the night or of the darkness. So then let us not sleep, as others do, but let us keep awake and be sober. 
>
> (1 Thessalonians 5:1–6 ESV)

The image of a thief here emphasizes suddenness. The day comes without warning for those who are spiritually asleep. This is different from mere speed, though the two concepts belong together. The day of the Lord arrives entirely unannounced, and once it hits, it does not linger. Paul is not handing the Thessalonians a date to circle on a calendar (see also Matthew 24:36), but giving them pastoral guidance for daily living. It is the reminder of Ephesians 6:10–20.

At the same time, Paul guards against the opposite mistake of panic. Some in Thessalonica had been deeply shaken by a report, perhaps an early forged letter or a misunderstood teaching, claiming that "the day of the Lord has come" (2 Thessalonians 2:2). Paul does not tell them the day is far away; rather, he clarifies that it has not yet arrived because a specific sequence of events must come first: "the day will not come, unless the rebellion comes first, and the man of lawlessness is revealed" (2 Thessalonians 2:3).

Paul holds these two truths in perfect tension. The day is genuinely near, yet a real historical sequence of events must unfold before it arrives. That is the same tension we find in Revelation 1:1–3. The phrases "must soon take place" and "the time is near" do not mean everything has already been completed, nor do they mean nothing must happen first. It means that the sovereign process is fully underway, moving toward a certain end on God’s timetable, not ours.

It is worth noticing that early Christians read these passages with this watchful posture. The *Didache*, an early church manual from the late first or early second century, explicitly instructs believers to watch over their lives because they do not know the hour of the Lord’s coming. It openly expects a final apostasy, the appearance of a world deceiver, and the Lord’s return in glory.[^7] Justin Martyr, writing in the middle of the second century, stated that he and many other orthodox believers expected a literal thousand-year reign of Christ on earth in a restored Jerusalem, though he honestly acknowledged that not every faithful Christian agreed.[^8] Irenaeus taught that a final opponent of God would desolate the earth before Christ’s visible return, a framework closely mirrored by Hippolytus.[^9] This expectation was not a novelty invented in recent centuries. Watchful nearness, a final rebellion, Christ’s physical return, and His earthly kingdom formed the foundation of Christian hope very early in the church.[^10]

One more passage often enters this discussion. Peter writes to early believers who wondered why the Lord had not yet returned: "with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (2 Peter 3:8). Peter is not redefining what is near, and we should not use this verse as an excuse to trivialize the delay. His point comes in the very next verse: "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you" (2 Peter 3:9). What looks like an agonizing delay to us is actually the mercy of God. It is patience with a profound purpose: that people may reach repentance. God is never late, and he is never bound to our calendars.

In summary, John’s use of the words "*soon*" and "*near*" invites us to view history through two distinct perspectives. Far from being a simple calendar date or a failed prediction, these opening markers reveal a sophisticated link to ancient prophecy while offering deep reassurance that a divine plan is actively unfolding. It serves as a compelling reminder that the history revealed in Scripture is not a series of random, chaotic events, but a purposeful narrative moving toward the definitive climax recorded in Revelation. Consequently, because the last days are already here, the events of Revelation must be understood throughout history as always being near. This reality calls Christians to a posture of constant readiness, knowing that when these events do unfold, they will be carried out suddenly and quickly.

Revelation 1:2 also tells us who John is and why he is writing. He is a **witness**. He is not repeating rumors or sharing his own ideas; he is testifying to what he saw. The early church understood this John to be the apostle John, one of Jesus’ original twelve disciples. Justin Martyr identified him this way, and Irenaeus placed the vision near the end of Emperor Domitian’s reign.

Why John is writing is disclosed in verse 9. Here we get a glimpse of John’s identity and pastoral heart. He writes from the island of Patmos, where he was because of "*the word of God and the testimony of Jesus.*" Yet he does not speak from a distance or above the struggles of the churches. He calls himself their "*brother and partner in the tribulation and the kingdom and the patient endurance that are in Jesus.*" By this stage of life, John, most likely in his eighties, writes as a faithful and experienced pastor who knows hardship firsthand. He shares this vision because he deeply loves these believers and wants to strengthen them to endure faithfully in Christ.

In this prologue we also find the first of Revelation’s seven explicit blessings, or beatitudes. Revelation 1:3 blesses not only the person who reads the prophecy aloud, but also the congregation that hears it and keeps what it says.

The wording reflects how Revelation was first received by the churches. One person would read the letter publicly while the gathered church listened. This was also Paul’s practice for his letters; he instructed that they be read aloud in the churches (Col. 4:16; 1 Thess. 5:27).

Revelation, then, is not meant merely for private study. It is a message for the worshiping community—heard together, understood together, and obeyed together. The church receives its blessing not simply by listening, but by keeping "*what is written in it.*"

Finally, notice the urgent reason given: "*for the time is near.*" Centuries earlier, Daniel was told to "*shut up the words and seal the book, until the time of the end*" (Daniel 12:4). John is told the exact opposite at the close of this book: "*Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book, for the time is near*" (Revelation 22:10). The book remains wide open for us because the time is near, and Christ himself repeats a promise that should strike anticipation into our hearts: "***I am coming soon***" (Revelation 22:7, 12, 20). Amen!

## Application

### Putting the Scriptures into Practice

Reading the Bible requires the active help of the Holy Spirit. Ask God to guide your reading of Revelation. Pray that he will not only grant intellectual understanding, but show you how to use what you learn to encourage others. I highly suggest reading the book aloud in large sections several times before focusing on individual details. Whenever possible, do this in community with friends and family! In fact, a complete reading of Revelation takes just over an hour.

Also consider journaling your reflections and spending time praying through them. Let the urgency of the message shape your requests in two distinct ways:

1. **Praying for Christ's Return:** The certain promise of Christ's return should inspire an expectant hope within us. Ask God to help you study Revelation in a manner that moves you to actively pray for the final restoration of all things and the visible return of our King.
2. **Praying for the Persecuted Church:** The extreme pressures faced by the first-century churches, including intense persecution, systemic hardship, and the literal threat of death, are not relics of the past. They remain a dangerous daily reality for millions of believers around the globe today. This call to spiritual alertness should stir a deep commitment in us to intercede regularly for our global brothers and sisters who are standing firm in the face of ongoing trials. ### Reflection and Discussion Questions

1. **Hearing in Community:** Revelation was written to be read aloud in a corporate gathering. How does changing your approach from a private reading to a shared reading affect the way you absorb its message? What are the practical challenges to reading Scripture "in community" in our modern context, and how can we overcome them?
2. **Holding Fast:** Ask yourself what you can apply from your reading: What do you think the Holy Spirit is telling you to hold onto today? Based on the absolute certainty of God’s sovereign timetable, what specific promises or truths do you need to actively guard and cherish in your current circumstances?
3. **Remaining Faithful:** Where is Christ asking you to remain faithful right now? Are there areas in your work, family, or personal life where the pressure to quiet your witness or match the habits of the culture feels most intense?
4. **Divided Allegiance:** Where are you tempted to give your ultimate trust, worship, or allegiance to something else? In what ways do modern subtle distractions—such as economic safety, career progress, or political identities—quietly pull our security away from the absolute sovereignty of God?
5. **Wakeful Posture:** If the biblical markers "soon" and "near" represent divine certainty and imminent velocity rather than a human calendar countdown, how should that framework alter your daily priorities? What is the practical difference between living in a state of speculative prediction versus a state of wakeful, sober expectancy?

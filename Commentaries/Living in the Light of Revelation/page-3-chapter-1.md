# Chapter 1

## Prologue

### Revelation 1:1–3

> 1 The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things that must soon take place. He made it known by sending his angel to his servant John, 2 who bore witness to the word of God and to the testimony of Jesus Christ, even to all that he saw. 3 Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear, and who keep what is written in it, for the time is near.

**Verse 1** *“The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things that must soon take place.”*

The opening verse invites us to slow down. This revelation of Jesus Christ comes from God the Father. That alone tells us that we are standing on holy ground. We are reading, and hearing, a revelation about God himself in the person of his Son, given to us by the Father. What a privilege this is, and it is one reason every Christian should be encouraged to read this book!

But a question rises almost immediately: What does John mean when he says that these things “must soon take place”? We are now in A.D. 2026. Some of Revelation’s themes were already unfolding in John’s day, but much of what the book describes has plainly not yet happened. We should not avoid that question. It is the elephant in the room, and Scripture gives us good reasons not to be troubled by it.

The phrase translated “soon” is *en tachei*. Its underlying Greek word can carry the sense of “soon,” “quickly,” or “without delay.”¹ The New Testament uses the phrase in more than one way. In Acts 12:7, an angel tells Peter to get up “quickly”; in Romans 16:20 and 1 Timothy 3:14, it is rendered “soon.” So the word itself does not settle every question about timing since it may have more than one meaning.

John Walvoord and other interpreters have suggested that, in Revelation 1:1, the phrase emphasizes how swiftly the events will unfold once God begins them, rather than telling us when they must begin. Walvoord described it as “a rapidity of execution after the beginning takes place.”² That is a fair reading. Yet we do not need to rest the whole matter on one Greek phrase. We can also look at how Scripture speaks of God’s promises in general. 

Consider Jesus’ parable of the persistent widow. He told it so that his disciples would “always pray and not lose heart” (Luke 18:1), and he concluded with these words:

> 6 And the Lord said, “Hear what the unrighteous judge says. 7 And will not God give justice to his elect, who cry to him day and night? Will he delay long over them? 8 I tell you, he will give justice to them *speedily*. Nevertheless, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?”
>
> (Luke 18:6–8 ESV; *italics mine*)

The widow does wait. From her point of view, the answer feels slow. Yet Jesus asks, “Will he delay long over them?” and says that God will give justice “speedily,” using the same phrase found in Revelation 1:1. Both things are true: God’s people experience waiting, but God is neither slow nor hesitant. Notice, too, where Jesus takes the story; to the coming of the Son of Man and to whether he will find faith on the earth. The promise of speed stands beside a call to keep praying and not lose heart.

Paul speaks in the same way: “The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet” (Rom. 16:20). Satan’s decisive defeat was won at the cross, though his final destruction still awaits Christ’s return (Rev. 20:10). Even so, Paul says “soon.” Habakkuk likewise says, “If it seems slow, wait for it” (Hab. 2:3), and Hebrews applies that promise to Christians: “the coming one will come and will not delay” (Heb. 10:37).

Therefore the use “soon” soon in Revelation 3 should be understood to mean how God will act when He orchestrates its events.

There is another detail that is easy to miss. When John says that God gave this revelation “to show to his servants the things that must soon take place,” he echoes Daniel 2. God revealed to Nebuchadnezzar what would happen “in the latter days” (Dan. 2:28). In the Greek Old Testament, Daniel uses *ha dei genesthai*—“what must take place”—the same words John uses in Revelation 1:1.³ But John adds something: these things must take place *soon*. What Daniel saw as still ahead, John announces has drawn near. The last days have begun.

That fits the testimony of the rest of the New Testament. At Pentecost, Peter said the outpouring of the Spirit fulfilled God’s promise for “the last days” (Acts 2:17). Hebrews says that God has spoken by his Son “in these last days” (Heb. 1:2). John himself wrote that because many antichrists had already come, “it is the last hour” (1 John 2:18). From Pentecost onward, the apostles understood themselves to be living in the last days. The entire period between Christ’s first and second comings—including our own day—is described that way in Scripture.

So when John writes “soon,” he is telling the churches that the final drama is already underway. They are not waiting for the curtain to rise. The play has begun, and it is moving toward its final scene.

This helps us understand why chapters 2 and 3 matter so much. The seven churches in Asia faced persecution, spiritual compromise, pressure to participate in emperor worship, and the danger of growing lukewarm. Those were not hypothetical problems. The pressures that will reach their climax at the end were already at the door, and they have never gone away. In every generation, the church is tempted to give ultimate allegiance, worship, or trust to something other than Christ. Revelation was written to prepare us for exactly that.

The Old Testament prophets speak in a similar way. Isaiah, Ezekiel, Joel, and Zephaniah all said that the day of the Lord was “near” (Isa. 13:6; Ezek. 30:3; Joel 1:15; Zeph. 1:7). Zephaniah even called it “near and hastening fast” (Zeph. 1:14). Often those warnings were fulfilled in judgments that fell in the prophets’ own day, yet the same language also points ahead to the final day of the Lord. This is not failed prophecy. It is how God speaks about his judgment: it is near, it is certain, and it presses on the conscience. It calls us to live watchfully, not to calculate dates.

Paul takes up the same theme in his first letter to the Thessalonians:

> 1 Now concerning the times and the seasons, brothers, you have no need to have anything written to you. 2 *For you yourselves are fully aware that the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night.* 3 While people are saying, “There is peace and security,” *then sudden destruction will come* upon them as labor pains come upon a pregnant woman, and they will not escape. 4 *But you are not in darkness, brothers, for that day to surprise you like a thief.* 5 For you are all children of light, children of the day. We are not of the night or of the darkness. 6 So then let us not sleep, as others do, but let us keep awake and be sober. 7 For those who sleep, sleep at night, and those who get drunk, are drunk at night. 8 But since we belong to the day, let us be sober, having put on the breastplate of faith and love, and for a helmet the hope of salvation.
>
> (1 Thessalonians 5:1–8 ESV; *italics mine*)

The thief image emphasizes suddenness. The day comes without warning for those who are asleep. That is different from speed, though the two belong together: the day of the Lord arrives unannounced, and it does not linger. Paul is not handing the Thessalonians a date to circle on a calendar (see also Matt. 24:36). He is telling them how to live: “keep awake and be sober” (v. 6), putting on “the breastplate of faith and love” (v. 8). Believers who are watching will not be surprised.

At the same time, Paul guards against the opposite mistake. Some in Thessalonica had been shaken by a report—perhaps a forged letter or a misunderstood teaching—that “the day of the Lord has come” (2 Thess. 2:2). Paul does not say the day cannot be near. He says it has not yet arrived, and he points to what must happen first: “that day will not come, unless the rebellion comes first, and the man of lawlessness is revealed” (v. 3).

Paul holds two truths together. The day is genuinely near, and a real sequence of events must come before it. That is the same tension we find in Revelation 1:1. “Must soon take place” does not mean “everything has already been completed,” and it does not mean “nothing must happen first.” It means that the process is underway and moving toward a certain end on God’s timetable, not ours.

It is worth noticing that early Christians read these passages with similar watchfulness. The *Didache*, an early church manual, tells believers to watch over their lives because they do not know the hour of the Lord’s coming.⁴ It also expects a rebellion, the appearance of the deceiver, and the Lord’s return in glory. Justin Martyr, writing in the middle of the second century, said that he and many other believers expected a thousand-year reign in Jerusalem, though he honestly acknowledged that not every faithful Christian agreed.⁵ Irenaeus taught that the antichrist would come before Christ’s return, as did Hippolytus.⁶ This was not a novelty invented in recent centuries. Watchful nearness, a coming rebellion, Christ’s return, and his kingdom formed the shape of Christian hope very early in the church.

One more passage often enters this discussion. Peter writes to believers who wondered why the Lord had not yet returned: “with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day” (2 Pet. 3:8). Peter is not defining “soon,” and we should not stretch this verse into permission to call any delay “soon.” His point comes in the next verse: “The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you” (v. 9). What looks like delay to us is not slowness to God. It is patience, and his patience has a purpose: that people may reach repentance. God is not late, and he is not bound to our calendar.

In the end, the elephant in the room is not an error but an invitation. John is not handing us a failed prediction. He is giving us armor. He knew his readers lived in a broken world where trials and dangers would continue until Christ’s return, and into that world he speaks one small word: “soon.” It is not a word meant to make us mark a calendar. It tells us that the last days have begun, the story is moving, and God is in control of where it is headed. "Soon" in its finality is calling us to arm ourselves with trust in God, to lean on Him in our trials, and to fix our eyes on the city of Revelation 21 and 22, where God makes His home with his people and wipes "every tear from their eyes" (Rev. 21:4).

**Verse 2** *“…who bore witness to the word of God and to the testimony of Jesus Christ, even to all that he saw.”*

Verse 2 tells us who John is and what he is doing. He is a witness. He is not passing along rumors or offering clever speculation. He is testifying to what he saw. The early church identified this John as the apostle John, one of the Twelve. Justin Martyr said so plainly, and Irenaeus likewise placed the vision near the end of Domitian’s reign.⁷

In verse 9 we get another hint that this is the apostle John and not another person. John tells us that he was on the island of Patmos “on account of the word of God and the testimony of Jesus.” He calls himself “your brother and partner in the tribulation and the kingdom and the patient endurance that are in Jesus.” This is a pastor speaking to people he knows and loves, not an outsider addressing strangers. Tribulation, kingdom, and patient endurance—those three words summarize the life of the church in the last days.

**Verse 3** *“Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear, and who keep what is written in it, for the time is near.”*

This is the first of seven blessings in Revelation (1:3; 14:13; 16:15; 19:9; 20:6; 22:7; 22:14), and it comes before anything else. Notice the pattern: one person reads aloud, and the others hear. Revelation was written to be read when the church gathered, just as Paul expected his letters to be shared among the churches (see Col. 4:16). The first way to receive this book, then, is together, with our ears as much as with our eyes.

But reading and hearing are not enough. The blessing belongs to those who “keep what is written in it.” To keep means to guard and obey. Revelation is not merely a puzzle to solve. It is a word to hold onto. The faithfulness Christ asks of the seven churches in chapters 2 and 3 depends on it.

Finally, notice the reason: “for the time is near.” Daniel was told to “shut up the words and seal the book, until the time of the end” (Dan. 12:4). John is told the opposite at the close of Revelation: “Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book, for the time is near” (Rev. 22:10). The book remains open because the time is near, and Christ himself repeats the promise: “I am coming soon” (Rev. 22:7, 12, 20).

What does that look like in practice? Read Revelation aloud several times. Read it in large sections, allowing the whole message to wash over you before you begin breaking it down. Read it with others whenever you can. Then ask a simple question: What is this calling me to hold onto? Where is Christ asking me to remain faithful? Where am I tempted to give my trust to something else?

That is how we keep what is written. It is also how we fix our eyes on the city of Revelation 21 and 22, where God makes his home with his people and “wipes every tear from their eyes” (Rev. 21:4).

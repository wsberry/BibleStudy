# Chapter 1

## Prologue

### Revelation 1:1–3

> 1 The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things that must soon take place. He made it known by sending his angel to his servant John, 2 who bore witness to the word of God and to the testimony of Jesus Christ, even to all that he saw. 3 Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear, and who keep what is written in it, for the time is near.

------

**Verse 1** *“The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things that must soon take place.”*

The opening verse of this book invites us to slow down and listen closely. This revelation of Jesus Christ comes directly from God the Father. That truth alone tells us that we are standing on holy ground. We are reading and hearing a disclosure about God himself in the person of his Son, given to us by the Father.

Imagine what receiving this message must have been like for the apostle John. At the time of this vision, he was living in exile on the rocky island of Patmos. He was “the disciple whom Jesus loved” (John 13:23). He had shared meals with Jesus before the crucifixion and had seen him alive in the resurrection. Yet, only a few verses later, when John sees his risen and glorified King, he falls at his feet “as though dead” (Revelation 1:17). We are truly dealing with holy mysteries here that are to be greatly treasured!

A difficult question rises almost immediately in this first verse: *What does John mean when he says that these things “must soon take place”?* We are living in the twenty-first century, and while some of Revelation’s themes were already unfolding in John’s day, much of what the book describes has plainly not yet happened. We should not avoid this question. It is the elephant in the room for many readers. Yet, Scripture gives us good reasons not to be troubled by this timeline, but to see it as an encouragement to live with watchfulness.

The phrase translated “soon” or “shortly” in most English Bibles is *en tachei*. This underlying Greek root can carry two meanings: chronological imminence ("soon" or "without delay") or qualitative velocity ("quickly" or "with swiftness"). The New Testament uses this phrase in both ways. In Acts 12:7, an angel commands the sleeping Peter to rise “quickly”; conversely, in Romans 16:20 and 1 Timothy 3:14, it is rendered “soon” or “shortly” to describe an impending timeframe.

While modern dispensationalists like John Walvoord^2^ have popularized the view that *en tachei* in Revelation 1:1 emphasizes the swiftness of events once God initiates them, describing it as “a rapidity of execution after the beginning takes place,” this structural interpretation is not exclusive to dispensationalism. Long before the emergence of pretribulational systems, a significant lineage of historic futurists, classical Greek scholars, and commentators held the belief that the text signifies a sudden and explosive velocity of execution once the prophetic timeline is initiated.

For example, nineteenth-century classical scholars like E.W. Bullinger and Walter Scott argued that *en tachei* describes the sudden, unhindered *manner* of fulfillment rather than a localized first-century calendar expectation. The renowned New Testament grammarian A.T. Robertson similarly noted that words sharing this root emphasize swiftness of motion, highlighting the etymological link to velocity rather than a simple delay factor. Modern expositors such as John MacArthur and David Guzik have carried this forward, demonstrating that when these end-time judgments finally hit history, they will unfold in rapid, uninterrupted succession like birth pains.

From a historical premillennial perspective, this language of chronological proximity is believed to be intentionally designed by the Holy Spirit to keep every generation of the Church in a state of wakeful expectancy. As John Piper notes, it forces us to live with the constant reality that Christ is always "at the gates".

This linguistic clarity is vital. It untangles the text from the strictures of preterism, which incorrectly insists that all these events had to be entirely fulfilled in a.d. 70. At the same time, it avoids the convoluted structural escapes of pretribulational dispensationalism. We do not need to rest the whole matter on a single Greek phrase, nor do we need to invent a secret, two-stage return of Christ to rescue the Church before the Tribulation. Instead, we look at how Scripture constructs prophetic certainty. The structural parallel to Revelation 1:1 is found in the Greek Septuagint translation of Daniel 2, where things that "must take place" (*ha dei genesthai*) are unveiled.

This text serves as a solemn warning to a Church called to endure, suffer, and bear an unwavering witness under the pressure of a hostile world system until the literal, post-tribulational appearance of her King. Pretribulational efforts to locate a secret escape hatch for the Church in texts like Revelation 3:10 falter under close exegesis. As John Piper observes, God’s promise to "keep us from the hour of trial" does not imply a physical removal from the world via a pre-tribulation rapture. Rather, matching Jesus' exact priestly prayer in John 17:15, where he asks the Father to "keep them from the evil one" (*tereo ek*), it is a glorious guarantee that God will supernaturally preserve and guard our faith *through* the fires of the trial.

When Christ does return at the end of the age, the Rapture will occur as a singular, triumphant event. Using the classical Greek concept of *apantesis* found in 1 Thessalonians 4:17, the Church will rise to meet the descending Lord in mid-air. We will act as a grand, victorious delegation that immediately escorts our King down to Earth to establish his literal millennial throne.

Consider Jesus’ parable of the persistent widow. He told it so that his disciples would “always pray and not lose heart” (Luke 18:1), and he concluded with these words:

> 6 And the Lord said, “Hear what the unrighteous judge says. 7 And will not God give justice to his elect, who cry to him day and night? Will he delay long over them? 8 I tell you, he will give justice to them *speedily*. Nevertheless, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?”
>
> (Luke 18:6–8 ESV)

The widow does wait. From her human point of view, the answer feels slow. Yet Jesus asks, “Will he delay long over them?” and says that God will give justice “speedily,” using the very same phrase found in Revelation 1:1. Both things are true: God’s people experience a season of waiting, but God is neither slow nor hesitant. Notice where Jesus takes the story: straight to the coming of the Son of Man and to whether he will find faith on the earth. The promise of speed stands right beside a pastoral call to keep praying and not lose heart.

Paul speaks in the same way to the believers in Rome: “The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet” (Romans 16:20). Satan’s decisive defeat was won at the cross, though his final destruction awaits Christ’s return (Revelation 20:10). Even so, Paul uses the word “soon.” The prophet Habakkuk likewise says, “If it seems slow, wait for it” (Habakkuk 2:3), and the writer of Hebrews applies that promise directly to Christians: “the coming one will come and will not delay” (Hebrews 10:37). Therefore, the use of “soon” in Revelation should be understood to mean how decisively God will act when he orchestrates these final events.

There is another detail that is easy to miss. When John says that God gave this revelation “to show to his servants the things that must soon take place,” he echoes Daniel 2. God revealed to King Nebuchadnezzar what would happen “in the latter days” (Daniel 2:28). In the Greek Old Testament, Daniel uses the words *ha dei genesthai*—“what must take place”—the exact phrase John chooses in Revelation 1:1. But John adds a crucial word: these things must take place *soon*. What Daniel saw as a distant reality, John announces has drawn near. The last days have officially begun.

This fits the uniform testimony of the rest of the New Testament. At Pentecost, Peter said the outpouring of the Spirit fulfilled God’s promise for “the last days” (Acts 2:17). The book of Hebrews says that God has spoken to us by his Son “in these last days” (Hebrews 10:37). John himself wrote in his first epistle that because many antichrists had already come, “it is the last hour” (1 John 2:18). From Pentecost onward, the apostles understood themselves to be living in the final era of redemptive history. The entire period between Christ’s first and second comings, including our own day, is described that way in Scripture.

So when John writes “soon,” he is telling the churches that the final drama is already underway. They are not waiting for the curtain to rise. The play has begun, and it is moving steadily toward its final scene.

This perspective helps us understand why chapters 2 and 3 matter so much to us practically. The seven historical churches in Asia faced intense persecution, spiritual compromise, social pressure to participate in emperor worship, and the subtle danger of growing lukewarm. Those were not hypothetical problems. The dark pressures that will reach their climax at the very end of history were already knocking at their door, and they have never gone away. In every generation, the church is tempted to give its ultimate allegiance, worship, or trust to something other than Jesus Christ. Revelation was written to prepare us for exactly that struggle.

The Old Testament prophets speak in this same dual way. Isaiah, Ezekiel, Joel, and Zephaniah all declared that the day of the Lord was “near” (Isaiah 13:6; Ezekiel 30:3; Joel 1:15; Zephaniah 1:7). Zephaniah even called it “near and hastening fast” (Zephaniah 1:14). Often those warnings were fulfilled in localized judgments that fell in the prophets’ own lifetimes, yet the same language points ahead to the final cosmic day of the Lord. This is not failed prophecy. It is simply how God speaks about his judgment: it is near, it is certain, and it presses upon the human conscience. It calls us to live watchfully today, not to calculate dates for tomorrow.

Paul takes up this exact theme in his first letter to the Thessalonians:

> 1 Now concerning the times and the seasons, brothers, you have no need to have anything written to you. 2 *For you yourselves are fully aware that the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night.* 3 While people are saying, “There is peace and security,” *then sudden destruction will come* upon them as labor pains come upon a pregnant woman, and they will not escape. 4 *But you are not in darkness, brothers, for that day to surprise you like a thief.* 5 For you are all children of light, children of the day. We are not of the night or of the darkness. 6 So then let us not sleep, as others do, but let us keep awake and be sober.
>
> (1 Thessalonians 5:1–6 ESV)

The image of a thief emphasizes suddenness. The day comes without warning for those who are spiritually asleep. This is different from mere speed, though the two concepts belong together: the day of the Lord arrives entirely unannounced, and once it hits, it does not linger. Paul is not handing the Thessalonians a date to circle on a calendar (see also Matthew 24:36). He is giving them a pastoral blueprint for daily living: “keep awake and be sober,” putting on the armor of faith, love, and hope. Believers who are actively watching will not be caught off guard.

At the same time, Paul guards against the opposite mistake of panic. Some in Thessalonica had been deeply shaken by a report, perhaps an early forged letter or a misunderstood teaching, claiming that “the day of the Lord has come” (2 Thessalonians 2:2). Paul does not tell them the day is far away; rather, he clarifies that it has not yet arrived because a specific sequence of events must come first: “that day will not come, unless the rebellion comes first, and the man of lawlessness is revealed” (2 Thessalonians 2:3).

Paul holds these two truths in perfect tension. The day is genuinely near, yet a real historical sequence of events must unfold before it arrives. That is the same healthy tension we find in Revelation 1:1. The phrase “must soon take place” does not mean everything has already been completed, nor does it mean nothing must happen first. It means that the sovereign process is fully underway, moving toward a certain end on God’s timetable, not ours.

It is worth noticing that early Christians read these passages with this exact, watchful posture. The *Didache*, an early church manual from the late first or early second century, explicitly instructs believers to watch over their lives because they do not know the hour of the Lord’s coming. It openly expects a final apostasy, the appearance of a world deceiver, and the Lord’s return in glory. Justin Martyr, writing in the middle of the second century, stated that he and many other orthodox believers expected a literal thousand-year reign in a restored Jerusalem, though he honestly acknowledged that not every faithful Christian agreed. Irenaeus taught that the antichrist would desolate the earth before Christ’s visible return, a framework closely mirrored by Hippolytus. This post-tribulational expectation was not a novelty invented in recent centuries. Watchful nearness, a final rebellion, Christ’s physical return, and his earthly kingdom formed the bedrock of Christian hope very early in the church.

One more passage often enters this discussion. Peter writes to early believers who wondered why the Lord had not yet returned: “with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day” (2 Peter 3:8). Peter is not redefining the word “soon,” and we should not use this verse as an excuse to trivialize the delay. His point comes in the very next verse: “The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you” (2 Peter 3:9). What looks like an agonizing delay to us is actually the mercy of God. It is patience with a profound purpose: that people may reach repentance. God is never late, and he is never bound to our calendars.

In the end, this timeline question is not an error to be explained away; it is a pastoral invitation. John is not handing us a failed prediction. He is giving us armor. He knew his readers lived in a broken world where trials, temptations, and dangers would continue until Christ’s return. Into that precise world, he speaks one comforting word: “soon”. It is not a word meant to help us calculate a date. It tells us that the last days are moving, the story is unfolding, and God is in absolute control of where it is headed. This word calls us to arm ourselves with trust, to lean heavily on him in our present trials, and to fix our eyes on the final city of Revelation 21 and 22, where God makes his home with his people and wipes every single tear from their eyes.

**Verse 2** *“…who bore witness to the word of God and to the testimony of Jesus Christ, even to all that he saw.”*

Verse 2 tells us exactly who John is and what he is doing in this book. He is a witness. He is not passing along secondhand rumors or offering clever human speculation. He is testifying to what he physically saw. The early church identified this author as the apostle John, one of the original Twelve. Justin Martyr said so plainly, and Irenaeus likewise placed the reception of this vision near the end of the Roman Emperor Domitian’s reign.

In verse 9, we get another clear hint that this is the apostle John speaking to us, rather than an detached outsider. He tells us that he was on the island of Patmos “on account of the word of god and the testimony of Jesus”. He addresses his readers as “your brother and partner in the tribulation and the kingdom and the patient endurance that are in Jesus”. This is a seasoned pastor speaking to people he deeply knows and loves. Tribulation, kingdom, and patient endurance—these three realities beautifully summarize the calling of the church in the last days.

**Verse 3** *“Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear, and who keep what is written in it, for the time is near.”*

This is the first of seven explicit blessings, or beatitudes, found in the book of Revelation, and it meets us right at the front door. Notice the community pattern: one person reads aloud, and the others listen together. Revelation was originally written to be read when the local church gathered for worship, just as Paul expected his letters to be shared corporately among the congregations. The primary way to receive this book, then, is together, with our ears as much as with our eyes.

But reading and hearing are only the beginning. The promised blessing belongs strictly to those who “keep what is written in it”. To keep means to guard, cherish, and obey. Revelation is not merely a complicated puzzle to solve. It is a living word to hold onto. The gritty faithfulness that Christ asks of the seven historical churches in chapters 2 and 3 depends entirely on this posture.

Finally, notice the urgent reason given: “for the time is near”. Centuries earlier, Daniel was told to “shut up the words and seal the book, until the time of the end” (Daniel 12:4). John is told the exact opposite at the close of this book: “Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book, for the time is near” (Revelation 22:10). The book remains wide open for us because the time is near, and Christ himself repeats the comforting promise: “I am coming soon” (Revelation 22:7, 12, 20).

What does this look like in our daily discipleship? Try reading Revelation aloud in large sections, allowing the grand storyline to wash over you before you focus on individual details. Whenever possible, read it in community. Then ask yourself a simple, pastoral question: What is this text calling me to hold onto today? Where is Christ asking me to remain faithful? Where am I tempted to give my ultimate trust to something else?

That is how we truly keep what is written. It is also how we fix our eyes on the final city of Revelation 21 and 22, where God makes his home with his people and forever wipes away every tear from their eyes.

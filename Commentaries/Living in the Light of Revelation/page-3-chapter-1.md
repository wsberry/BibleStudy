# Chapter 1

## Prologue

### Revelation 1:1–3

> 1 The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things that must soon take place. He made it known by sending his angel to his servant John, 2 who bore witness to the word of God and to the testimony of Jesus Christ, even to all that he saw. 3 Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear, and who keep what is written in it, for the time is near.

------

**Verse 1** *“The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things that must soon take place.”*

The opening verse of this book invites us to slow down and listen closely. This revelation of Jesus Christ comes directly from God the Father. That truth alone tells us that we are standing on holy ground. We are reading and hearing a disclosure about God himself in the person of his Son, given to us by the Father.

Imagine what receiving this message must have been like for the apostle John. At the time of this vision, he was living in exile on the island of Patmos. He was “the disciple whom Jesus loved” (John 13:23). He had shared meals with Jesus before the crucifixion and had fellowshipped with Him after the resurrection. Yet, only a few verses later in this first chapter of Revelation, when John sees his risen and glorified King, he falls at his feet “as though dead” (Revelation 1:17). We are truly dealing with holy mysteries here that are to be greatly treasured. Be sure to take time to pray and worship God as you read the mysteries of this book!

A difficult question rises almost immediately in this first verse: *What does John mean when he says that these things “must soon take place”?* We are living in the twenty-first century, and while some of Revelation’s themes were already unfolding in John’s day, much of what the book describes has plainly not yet happened. We should not avoid this question. It is the elephant in the room for many readers. Yet, Scripture gives us good reasons not to be troubled by the timeline that "soon" may seem to imply. Let's look at why this is the case and gain some clarity of what John very likely means.

The phrase translated “soon” or “shortly” in most English Bibles is *en tachei*[^1]. This underlying Greek root can carry two meanings: chronological imminence ("soon" or "without delay") or qualitative velocity ("quickly" or "with swiftness"). The New Testament uses this phrase in both ways. In Acts 12:7, an angel commands the sleeping Peter to rise “quickly”; conversely, in Romans 16:20 and 1 Timothy 3:14, it is rendered “soon” or “shortly” to describe an impending timeframe.

While modern dispensationalists like [John Walvoord](https://www.google.com/search?q=john+walvoord&kgmid=/m/05vy6vn)[^2] have popularized the view that *en tachei* in Revelation 1:1 emphasizes the swiftness of events once God initiates them, describing it as “a rapidity of execution after the beginning takes place,” this structural interpretation is not exclusive to dispensationalism. Long before the emergence of pretribulational systems, a significant lineage of historic futurists, classical Greek scholars, and commentators held the belief that the text signifies a sudden and explosive velocity of execution once the prophetic timeline is initiated.

For example, nineteenth-century classical scholars like [E.W. Bullinger](https://www.google.com/search?q=e.w.+bullinger&kgmid=/m/01mv6t) and [Walter Scott](https://www.google.com/search?q=walter+scott&kgmid=/m/06y8v) argued that *en tachei* describes the sudden, unhindered *manner* of fulfillment rather than a localized first-century calendar expectation[^2]. The renowned New Testament grammarian [A.T. Robertson](https://www.google.com/search?q=a.t.+robertson&kgmid=/m/02qksmp) similarly noted that words sharing this root emphasize swiftness of motion, highlighting the etymological link to velocity rather than a simple delay factor[^2]. Modern expositors such as [John MacArthur](https://www.google.com/search?q=john+macarthur&kgmid=/m/05pln3) and [David Guzik](https://www.google.com/search?q=david+guzik&kgmid=/m/05vkvjm) have carried this forward, demonstrating that when these end-time judgments are revealed in history, they will unfold in rapid, uninterrupted succession like birth pains[^2].

From a historical premillennial perspective, this language of chronological proximity is believed to be intentionally designed by the Holy Spirit to keep every generation of the Church in a state of wakeful expectancy. As John Piper notes, it forces us to live with the constant reality that Christ is always "at the gates"[^2]:

> Therefore, preparing your minds for action, and being sober-minded, set your hope fully on the grace that will be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ. (1 Book of Peter 1:13 ESV)
>
> “Stay dressed for action and keep your lamps burning, and be like men who are waiting for their master to come home from the wedding feast, so that they may open the door to him at once when he comes and knocks. Blessed are those servants whom the master finds awake when he comes. Truly, I say to you, he will dress himself for service and have them recline at table, and he will come and serve them. If he comes in the second watch, or in the third, and finds them awake, blessed are those servants! But know this, that if the master of the house had known at what hour the thief was coming, he would not have left his house to be broken into. You also must be ready, for the Son of Man is coming at an hour you do not expect.” (Gospel of Luke 12:35-40 ESV)

This linguistic clarity is essential to understanding what "soon" means. It untangles the book of Revelation as a whole from the strictures of preterism, which incorrectly insists that all these events had to be entirely fulfilled in a.d. 70. At the same time, it avoids the complex structural escapes of pretribulational dispensationalism. We do not need to rest the whole matter on a single Greek phrase, nor do we need to invent a secret, two-stage return of Christ to rescue the Church before the Tribulation. Instead, we look at how Scripture constructs its prophetic timeline.

A structural parallel to Revelation 1:1 is found in the Greek Septuagint translation of Daniel 2, where things that "must take place" (*ha dei genesthai*) are unveiled[^3]. The significance of this parallel is that the opening words of Revelation are a deliberate echo of Daniel 2 in the Greek Old Testament. By using the exact phrase *ha dei genesthai*, John is signaling to his readers that the mysterious end-time prophecies given to the prophet Daniel are finally being unlocked and fulfilled in the story of Jesus. Furthermore, the word ***dei*** (“must” or “it is necessary”) carries a heavy theological weight in apocalyptic literature. It implies *divine necessity*. John is using this parallel to comfort a persecuted early Church, reminding them that history is not spinning out of control. The trials they are facing are part and parcel of God's sovereign, predetermined timeline.

Pretribulational efforts to find a secret escape hatch for the Church in verses like Revelation 3:10 simply fall apart under close study. As John Piper observes, God’s promise to "keep us from the hour of trial" does not mean we will be physically removed from the world before the Tribulation[^8]. Instead, it mirrors Jesus' exact priestly prayer in John 17:15, where he asks the Father to "keep them from the evil one" (*tereo ek*)[^8]. This is not a promise of escape; it is a glorious guarantee that God will supernaturally preserve and guard our faith *through* the fires of that trial[^8].

When Jesus does return at the end of the age, his coming will be a single, triumphant event. This is beautifully illustrated by the Greek word for "meet" (*apantesis*) used in 1 Thessalonians 4:17[^9]:

> 13 But we do not want you to be uninformed, brothers, about those who are asleep, that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope. 14 For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so, through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep. 15 For this we declare to you by a word from the Lord, that we who are alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord, will not precede those who have fallen asleep. 16 For the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a cry of command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the sound of the trumpet of God. And the dead in Christ will rise first. 17 Then we who are alive, who are left, will be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air, and so we will always be with the Lord. 18 Therefore encourage one another with these words (1 Thessalonians 4:13-18)

In the ancient world, *apantesis* described a grand, victorious delegation that would go out to meet an approaching king and immediately escort him back into the city[^9]. That is the future of Christians living at the time of Christ's return. The Church will rise to meet the descending Lord in the air, acting as his royal escort down to Earth to establish his literal millennial throne[^9].

Consider Jesus’ parable of the persistent widow. He told it so that his disciples would “always pray and not lose heart” (Luke 18:1), and he concluded with these words:

> And the Lord said, “Hear what the unrighteous judge says. And will not God give justice to his elect, who cry to him day and night? Will he delay long over them? I tell you, he will give justice to them *speedily*. Nevertheless, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?”
>
> (Gospel of Luke 18:6–8 ESV)

The widow does wait. From her human point of view, the answer feels slow. Yet Jesus asks, “Will he delay long over them?” and says that God will give justice “speedily,” using the very same phrase found in Revelation 1:1. Both things are true: God’s people experience a season of waiting, but God is neither slow nor hesitant. Notice where Jesus takes the story: straight to the coming of the Son of Man and to whether he will find faith on the earth. The promise of speed stands right beside a pastoral call to keep praying and not lose heart.

Paul speaks in the same way to the believers in Rome: “The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet” (Romans 16:20). Satan’s decisive defeat was won at the cross, though his final destruction awaits Christ’s return (Revelation 20:10). Even so, Paul uses the word “soon.” The prophet Habakkuk likewise says, “If it seems slow, wait for it” (Habakkuk 2:3), and the writer of Hebrews applies that promise directly to Christians: “the coming one will come and will not delay” (Hebrews 10:37). Therefore, the use of “soon” in Revelation should be understood to mean how decisively God will act when he orchestrates these final events.

A less obvious point deserves attention in this discussion as well. When John states that God gave this revelation “to show to his servants the things that must soon take place,” he directly echoes Daniel 2. In that Old Testament passage, God revealed to King Nebuchadnezzar what would happen “in the latter days” (Daniel 2:28). In the ancient Greek translation of Daniel, the phrase used is *ha dei genesthai*—“what must take place.” John deliberately chooses this exact phrase in Revelation 1:1, but he adds a crucial word: *soon*. What Daniel viewed as a distant, future reality, John announces has now drawn very near.

This sense of fulfillment aligns perfectly with the consistent testimony of the rest of the New Testament. At Pentecost, Peter argued that the outpouring of the Holy Spirit fulfilled God’s promise for “the last days” (Acts 2:17). Similarly, the author of Hebrews notes that God has spoken to us by his Son “in these last days” (Hebrews 1:2). John himself wrote in his first letter that because opponents of Christ had already arrived, “it is the last hour” (1 John 2:18). From Pentecost onward, the apostles understood themselves to be living in the final era of human history. And this should be our understanding today.

Consequently, the word "soon" in Revelation 1:1 carries a dual purpose. First, it signifies that the drama of the final era has already been set in motion and is currently underway. Second, it implies urgency: as the events recorded in Revelation unfold in their respective eras, they will be carried out swiftly and without delay. Therefore, Christians are called to anticipate the return of Jesus as a reality that is always imminent.

This perspective explains why the letters to the historical churches in chapters 2 and 3 were recorded and why they still matter practically today. The dark pressures of persecution, spiritual compromise, emperor worship, and the danger of growing lukewarm were intensely real for those early followers of Christ. They were being called to remain faithful, endure great suffering, and face death if required. While these extreme trials may seem distant to many readers in the West, they represent a daily, dangerous reality for Christians in other parts of the world today. At the same time, the contemporary Western church faces its own subtle versions of these ancient struggles, particularly regarding where believers place their deepest allegiance. In the United States, for instance, many Christians tie their identity closely to political parties or leaders, such as the Republican Party and Donald Trump. This reality raises a fundamental question that Revelation was written to address: *Where does a Christian's ultimate allegiance, worship, and trust belong?* Scripture's answer is clear, it belongs exclusively to God.

The Old Testament prophets speak in this same dual way. Isaiah, Ezekiel, Joel, and Zephaniah all declared that the day of the Lord was “near” (Isaiah 13:6; Ezekiel 30:3; Joel 1:15; Zephaniah 1:7). Zephaniah even called it “near and hastening fast” (Zephaniah 1:14). Often those warnings were fulfilled in localized judgments that fell in the prophets’ own lifetimes, yet the same language points ahead to the final cosmic day of the Lord. This is not failed prophecy. It is simply how God speaks about his judgment: it is near, it is certain, and it presses upon the human conscience. It calls us to live watchfully today, not to calculate dates for tomorrow.

Paul takes up this exact theme in his first letter to the Thessalonians:

> 1 Now concerning the times and the seasons, brothers, you have no need to have anything written to you. 2 *For you yourselves are fully aware that the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night.* 3 While people are saying, “There is peace and security,” *then sudden destruction will come* upon them as labor pains come upon a pregnant woman, and they will not escape. 4 *But you are not in darkness, brothers, for that day to surprise you like a thief.* 5 For you are all children of light, children of the day. We are not of the night or of the darkness. 6 So then let us not sleep, as others do, but let us keep awake and be sober.
>
> (1 Thessalonians 5:1–6 ESV)

The image of a thief emphasizes suddenness. The day comes without warning for those who are spiritually asleep. This is different from mere speed, though the two concepts belong together: the day of the Lord arrives entirely unannounced, and once it hits, it does not linger. Paul is not handing the Thessalonians a date to circle on a calendar (see also Matthew 24:36). He is giving them a pastoral blueprint for daily living: “keep awake and be sober,” putting on the armor of faith, love, and hope. Believers who are actively watching will not be caught off guard.

At the same time, Paul guards against the opposite mistake of panic. Some in Thessalonica had been deeply shaken by a report, perhaps an early forged letter or a misunderstood teaching, claiming that “the day of the Lord has come” (2 Thessalonians 2:2). Paul does not tell them the day is far away; rather, he clarifies that it has not yet arrived because a specific sequence of events must come first: “the day will not come, unless the rebellion comes first, and the man of lawlessness is revealed” (2 Thessalonians 2:3).

Paul holds these two truths in perfect tension. The day is genuinely near, yet a real historical sequence of events must unfold before it arrives. That is the same healthy tension we find in Revelation 1:1. The phrase “must soon take place” does not mean everything has already been completed, nor does it mean nothing must happen first. It means that the sovereign process is fully underway, moving toward a certain end on God’s timetable, not ours.

It is worth noticing that early Christians read these passages with this exact, watchful posture. The *Didache*, an early church manual from the late first or early second century, explicitly instructs believers to watch over their lives because they do not know the hour of the Lord’s coming[^4]. It openly expects a final apostasy, the appearance of a world deceiver, and the Lord’s return in glory. Justin Martyr, writing in the middle of the second century, stated that he and many other orthodox believers expected a literal thousand-year reign in a restored Jerusalem, though he honestly acknowledged that not every faithful Christian agreed[^5]. Irenaeus taught that the antichrist would desolate the earth before Christ’s visible return, a framework closely mirrored by Hippolytus[^6]. This post-tribulational expectation was not a novelty invented in recent centuries. Watchful nearness, a final rebellion, Christ’s physical return, and his earthly kingdom formed the bedrock of Christian hope very early in the church.

One more passage often enters this discussion. Peter writes to early believers who wondered why the Lord had not yet returned: “with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day” (2 Book of Peter 3:8). Peter is not redefining the word “soon,” and we should not use this verse as an excuse to trivialize the delay. His point comes in the very next verse: “The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you” (2 Book of Peter 3:9). What looks like an agonizing delay to us is actually the mercy of God. It is patience with a profound purpose: that people may reach repentance. God is never late, and he is never bound to our calendars.

In the end, this timeline question is not an error to be explained away; it is a pastoral invitation. John is not handing us a failed prediction. He is giving us armor. He knew his readers lived in a broken world where trials, temptations, and dangers would continue until Christ’s return. Into that precise world, he speaks one comforting word: “soon”. It is not a word meant to help us calculate a date. It tells us that the last days are moving, the story is unfolding, and God is in absolute control of where it is headed. This word calls us to arm ourselves with trust, to lean heavily on him in our present trials, and to fix our eyes on the final city of Revelation 21 and 22, where God makes his home with his people and wipes every single tear from their eyes.

**Verse 2** *“…who bore witness to the word of God and to the testimony of Jesus Christ, even to all that he saw.”*

Verse 2 tells us exactly who John is and what he is doing in this book. He is a witness. He is not passing along secondhand rumors or offering clever human speculation. He is testifying to what he physically saw. The early church identified this author as the apostle John, one of the original Twelve. Justin Martyr said so plainly, and Irenaeus likewise placed the reception of this vision near the end of the Roman Emperor Domitian’s reign[^7].

In verse 9, we get another clear hint that this is the apostle John speaking to us, rather than an detached outsider. He tells us that he was on the island of Patmos “on account of the word of god and the testimony of Jesus”. He addresses his readers as “your brother and partner in the tribulation and the kingdom and the patient endurance that are in Jesus”. This is a seasoned pastor speaking to people he deeply knows and loves. Tribulation, kingdom, and patient endurance—these three realities beautifully summarize the calling of the church in the last days.

**Verse 3** *“Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear, and who keep what is written in it, for the time is near.”*

This is the first of seven explicit blessings, or beatitudes, found in the book of Revelation, and it meets us right at the front door. Notice the community pattern: one person reads aloud, and the others listen together. Revelation was originally written to be read when the local church gathered for worship, just as Paul expected his letters to be shared corporately among the congregations. The primary way to receive this book, then, is together, with our ears as much as with our eyes.

But reading and hearing are only the beginning. The promised blessing belongs strictly to those who “keep what is written in it”. To keep means to guard, cherish, and obey. Revelation is not merely a complicated puzzle to solve. It is a living word to hold onto. The gritty faithfulness that Christ asks of the seven historical churches in chapters 2 and 3 depends entirely on this posture.

Finally, notice the urgent reason given: “for the time is near”. Centuries earlier, Daniel was told to “shut up the words and seal the book, until the time of the end” (Daniel 12:4). John is told the exact opposite at the close of this book: “Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book, for the time is near” (Revelation 22:10). The book remains wide open for us because the time is near, and Christ himself repeats the comforting promise: “I am coming soon” (Revelation 22:7, 12, 20).

What does this look like in our daily discipleship? Try reading Revelation aloud in large sections, allowing the grand storyline to wash over you before you focus on individual details. Whenever possible, read it in community. Then ask yourself a simple, pastoral question: What is this text calling me to hold onto today? Where is Christ asking me to remain faithful? Where am I tempted to give my ultimate trust to something else?

That is how we truly keep what is written. It is also how we fix our eyes on the final city of Revelation 21 and 22, where God makes his home with his people and forever wipes away every tear from their eyes.

## Definitions



- **Amillennialism**: The theological perspective that rejects a literal future thousand-year kingdom of Jesus Christ on earth. Adherents interpret the millennium instead as a spiritual reality, identifying it either as Christ's current heavenly reign or His spiritual presence within the Church during the present age between His first and second comings.

- **Dispensationalism**: A systematic theological framework that views the historical unfolding of God's redemptive plan through distinct structural periods or administrations called economies (dispensations). A defining characteristic is the maintenance of a strict theological distinction between God's distinct purposes for national Israel and His purposes for the New Testament Church.

- **Historic Futurist**: An approach to biblical interpretation holding that the major prophetic visions and judgments in the book of Revelation describe literal historical occurrences that will take place at the end of the age. This perspective views the fulfillment of these events as future rather than entirely fulfilled in antiquity or understood as purely abstract moral symbols.

- **Historic Premillennialism**: The classical eschatological view held widely by the early Church fathers, asserting that Jesus Christ will return physically to the earth after a final period of intense testing. Following this post-tribulational return, Christ will establish a literal thousand-year theocratic kingdom on earth, during which the Church remains present on the field as a visible witness.

- **Millennial Kingdom**: The literal thousand-year earthly reign of Jesus Christ following His second advent. Prophesied extensively throughout the Old and New Testaments, this future era will be characterized by a perfect, righteous government, the absolute subjection of demonic forces, cosmic peace, and global economic and spiritual restoration.

- **Postmillennialism**: The eschatological viewpoint holding that the second coming of Christ will occur after a literal or figurative thousand-year era of widespread peace, righteousness, and global spiritual prosperity. This system predicts that the kingdom of God will be progressively realized in the present age through the triumphant spiritual influence of the Church preaching the gospel.

- **Preterism**: An interpretive approach to biblical prophecy holding that most or all of the predictions in the book of Revelation were already entirely fulfilled in the past. Preterists argue that these eschatological events were primarily realized during the early centuries of the Christian era, culminating specifically in the destruction of Jerusalem in a.d. 70.

- **Pretribulational Dispensationalism**: A specific variation of dispensationalism holding that Jesus Christ will secretly return to catch up (rapture) the Church into heaven prior to the beginning of the final seven-year period of world history. This framework separates the rapture from the visible second coming of Christ by a seven-year tribulation period.

- **Tribulation**: An intense, future period of unprecedented global distress, widespread spiritual conflict, severe persecution of the saints, and catastrophic divine judgments. According to prophetic Scripture, this tumultuous era immediately precedes the glorious, visible second coming of Jesus Christ to the earth.

# Chapter 1

## Prologue

### Revelation 1:1–3

> 1 The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things that must soon take place. He made it known by sending his angel to his servant John, 2 who bore witness to the word of God and to the testimony of Jesus Christ, even to all that he saw. 3 Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear, and who keep what is written in it, for the time is near.

The opening verse of this book invites us to slow down and listen closely. This revelation of Jesus Christ comes directly from God the Father. That truth alone tells us that we are standing on holy ground. We are reading and hearing a disclosure about God himself in the person of his Son, given to us by the Father.

Imagine what receiving this message must have been like for the apostle John. At the time of this vision, he was living in exile on the island of Patmos. He was “the disciple whom Jesus loved” (John 13:23). He had shared meals with Jesus before the crucifixion and had fellowshipped with Him after the resurrection. Yet, only a few verses later in this first chapter of Revelation, when John sees his risen and glorified King, he falls at his feet “as though dead” (Revelation 1:17). We are truly dealing with holy mysteries here that are to be greatly treasured. Be sure to take time to pray and worship God as you read the mysteries of this book!

Two difficult questions rise almost immediately in this prologue: *What does John mean when he says that these things “must soon take place” (v. 1), and what does he mean when he concludes that “the time is near” (v. 3)?* We are living in the twenty-first century, and while some of Revelation’s themes were already unfolding in John’s day, much of what the book describes has plainly not yet happened. We should not avoid these questions; they are the elephant in the room for many readers. Yet, Scripture gives us good reasons not to be troubled by the timeline that "soon" and "near" may seem to imply. Let's look at why this is the case and gain some clarity on what John very likely means.

The phrase translated “soon” or “shortly” in most English Bibles is *en tachei*. This underlying Greek root can carry two meanings: chronological imminence ("soon" or "without delay") or qualitative velocity ("quickly" or "with swiftness"). The New Testament uses this phrase in both ways. In Acts 12:7, an angel commands the sleeping Peter to rise “quickly”; conversely, in Romans 16:20 and 1 Timothy 3:14, it is rendered “soon” or “shortly” to describe an impending timeframe.[^1]

While it is popular to think this phrase only refers to a calendar countdown, a significant lineage of classical Greek scholars, commentators, and early church history readers held the belief that the text signifies a sudden and explosive velocity of execution once the prophetic timeline is initiated. It emphasizes the swiftness of events *once God begins them*—a rapid execution after the start takes place.

For example, nineteenth-century classical scholars like E.W. Bullinger and Walter Scott argued that *en tachei* describes the sudden, unhindered *manner* of fulfillment rather than a localized first-century calendar expectation. The renowned New Testament grammarian A.T. Robertson similarly noted that words sharing this root emphasize swiftness of motion, highlighting the etymological link to velocity rather than a simple delay factor. Modern expositors such as John MacArthur and David Guzik have carried this forward, demonstrating that when these end-time judgments are revealed in history, they will unfold in rapid, uninterrupted succession like birth pains.[^2]

Just as John uses *en tachei* to describe the *manner* of these events, he uses the Greek word ***engys*** (**ἐγγύς**) at the end of verse 3 to state that the time is “near”. In the New Testament, *engys* can signify physical proximity (being close by in space) or temporal proximity (being close by in time). In the context of prophecy, however, *engys* functions as a spiritual reality rather than a strict calendar countdown. It signals that the next major epoch on God's redemptive calendar has arrived and stands right at the threshold.[^10]

This combined language of chronological proximity (*engys*) and rapid execution (*en tachei*) is intentionally designed by the Holy Spirit to keep every generation of the Church in a state of wakeful expectancy. As John Piper notes,[^2] it forces us to live with the constant reality that Christ is always "at the gates":

> Therefore, preparing your minds for action, and being sober-minded, set your hope fully on the grace that will be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ. (1 Peter 1:13 ESV)
>
> “Stay dressed for action and keep your lamps burning, and be like men who are waiting for their master to come home from the wedding feast, so that they may open the door to him at once when he comes and knocks. Blessed are those servants whom the master finds awake when he comes. Truly, I say to you, he will dress himself for service and have them recline at table, and he will come and serve them. If he comes in the second watch, or in the third, and finds them awake, blessed are those servants! But know this, that if the master of the house had known at what hour the thief was coming, he would not have left his house to be broken into. You also must be ready, for the Son of Man is coming at an hour you do not expect.” (Luke 12:35-40 ESV)

This linguistic clarity is essential to understanding what "soon" and "near" mean. It untangles the book of Revelation as a whole from theories that incorrectly insist all these events had to be entirely fulfilled in the ancient past, such as around A.D. 70. At the same time, it avoids the need to construct highly complicated, multi-stage escape timelines that are nowhere explicitly stated in the text. We do not need to rest the whole matter on a single Greek phrase, nor do we need to look for a secret, hidden rescue of the Church before the final trial.[^8][^9] Instead, we simply look at how Scripture itself constructs its prophetic timeline.

A structural parallel to Revelation 1:1 is found in the Greek Septuagint translation of Daniel 2, where things that "must take place" (*ha dei genesthai*) are unveiled.[^3] The significance of this parallel is that the opening words of Revelation are a deliberate echo of Daniel 2 in the Greek Old Testament. By using the exact phrase *ha dei genesthai*, John is signaling to his readers that the mysterious end-time prophecies given to the prophet Daniel are finally being unlocked and fulfilled in the story of Jesus. Furthermore, the word ***dei*** (“must” or “it is necessary”) carries a heavy theological weight in apocalyptic literature. It implies *divine necessity*. John is using this parallel to comfort a persecuted early Church, reminding them that history is not spinning out of control. The trials they are facing are part and parcel of God's sovereign, predetermined timeline.

Another scripture that demonstrates what is meant by this prophetic timeline is Jesus’ parable of the persistent widow. He told it so that his disciples would “always pray and not lose heart” (Luke 18:1), and he concluded with these words:

> And the Lord said, “Hear what the unrighteous judge says. And will not God give justice to his elect, who cry to him day and night? Will he delay long over them? I tell you, he will give justice to them *speedily*. Nevertheless, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?”
>
> (Luke 18:6–8 ESV)

The widow does wait. From her human point of view, the answer feels slow. Yet Jesus asks, “Will he delay long over them?” and says that God will give justice “speedily,” using the very same phrase (*en tachei*) found in Revelation 1:1. Both things are true: God’s people experience a season of waiting, but God is neither slow nor hesitant. Notice where Jesus takes the story: straight to the coming of the Son of Man and to whether he will find faith on the earth. The promise of speed stands right beside a pastoral call to keep praying and not lose heart.

Paul speaks in the same way to the believers in Rome: “The God of peace will soon (*en tachei*) crush Satan under your feet” (Romans 16:20). Satan’s decisive defeat was won at the cross, though his final destruction awaits Christ’s return (Revelation 20:10). Even so, Paul uses the word “soon.” The prophet Habakkuk likewise says, “If it seems slow, wait for it” (Habakkuk 2:3), and the writer of Hebrews applies that promise directly to Christians: “the coming one will come and will not delay” (Hebrews 10:37). Therefore, the use of “soon” and "near" in Revelation should be understood to mean how decisively God will act when he orchestrates these final events.

A less obvious point deserves attention in this discussion as well. When John states that God gave this revelation “to show to his servants the things that must soon take place,” he directly echoes Daniel 2. In that Old Testament passage, God revealed to King Nebuchadnezzar what would happen “in the latter days” (Daniel 2:28). In the ancient Greek translation of Daniel, the phrase used is *ha dei genesthai*—“what must take place.” John deliberately chooses this exact phrase in Revelation 1:1, but he adds a crucial word: *soon*. What Daniel viewed as a distant, future reality, John announces has now drawn very near (*engys*).

This sense of fulfillment aligns perfectly with the consistent testimony of the rest of the New Testament. At Pentecost, Peter argued that the outpouring of the Holy Spirit fulfilled God’s promise for “the last days” (Acts 2:17). Similarly, the author of Hebrews notes that God has spoken to us by his Son “in these last days” (Hebrews 1:2). John himself wrote in his first letter that because opponents of Christ had already arrived, “it is the last hour” (1 John 2:18). From Pentecost onward, the apostles understood themselves to be living in the final era of human history. And this should be our understanding today.

Consequently, the words "soon" and "near" in the prologue carry a dual purpose. First, they signify that the drama of the final era has already been set in motion, meaning that the kingdom of God has drawn close enough to break into human history. Second, they imply urgency: as the events recorded in Revelation unfold in their respective eras, they will be carried out swiftly and without delay. Therefore, Christians are called to anticipate the return of Jesus as a reality that is always imminent.

This perspective explains why the letters to the historical churches in chapters 2 and 3 were recorded and why they still matter practically today. The dark pressures of persecution, spiritual compromise, emperor worship, and the danger of growing lukewarm were intensely real for those early followers of Christ. They were being called to remain faithful, endure great suffering, and face death if required. While these extreme trials may seem distant to many readers in the West, they represent a daily, dangerous reality for Christians in other parts of the world today.

At the same time, the contemporary Western church faces its own subtle versions of these ancient struggles, particularly regarding where believers place their deepest allegiance. In the United States, for instance, many Christians tie their identity closely to political parties or leaders, such as the Republican Party and Donald Trump. This reality raises a fundamental question that Revelation was written to address: *Where does a Christian's ultimate allegiance, worship, and trust belong?* Scripture's answer is clear: it belongs exclusively to God.

The Old Testament prophets speak in this same dual way. Isaiah, Ezekiel, Joel, and Zephaniah all declared that the day of the Lord was “near” (*engys* in the Septuagint) (Isaiah 13:6; Ezekiel 30:3; Joel 1:15; Zephaniah 1:7). Zephaniah even called it “near and hastening fast” (Zephaniah 1:14). Often those warnings were fulfilled in localized judgments that fell in the prophets’ own lifetimes, yet the same language points ahead to the final cosmic day of the Lord. This is not failed prophecy. It is simply how God speaks about his judgment: it is near, it is certain, and it presses upon the human conscience. It calls us to live watchfully today, not to calculate dates for tomorrow.

Paul takes up this exact theme in his first letter to the Thessalonians:

> 1 Now concerning the times and the seasons, brothers, you have no need to have anything written to you. 2 *For you yourselves are fully aware that the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night.* 3 While people are saying, “There is peace and security,” *then sudden destruction will come* upon them as labor pains come upon a pregnant woman, and they will not escape. 4 *But you are not in darkness, brothers, for that day to surprise you like a thief.* 5 For you are all children of light, children of the day. We are not of the night or of the darkness. 6 So then let us not sleep, as others do, but let us keep awake and be sober.
>
> (1 Thessalonians 5:1–6 ESV)

The image of a thief emphasizes suddenness. The day comes without warning for those who are spiritually asleep. This is different from mere speed, though the two concepts belong together: the day of the Lord arrives entirely unannounced, and once it hits, it does not linger. Paul is not handing the Thessalonians a date to circle on a calendar (see also Matthew 24:36). He is giving them a pastoral blueprint for daily living: “keep awake and be sober,” putting on the armor of faith, love, and hope. Believers who are actively watching will not be caught off guard.

At the same time, Paul guards against the opposite mistake of panic. Some in Thessalonica had been deeply shaken by a report, perhaps an early forged letter or a misunderstood teaching, claiming that “the day of the Lord has come” (2 Thessalonians 2:2). Paul does not tell them the day is far away; rather, he clarifies that it has not yet arrived because a specific sequence of events must come first: “the day will not come, unless the rebellion comes first, and the man of lawlessness is revealed” (2 Thessalonians 2:3).

Paul holds these two truths in perfect tension. The day is genuinely near, yet a real historical sequence of events must unfold before it arrives. That is the same healthy tension we find in Revelation 1:1–3. The phrases “must soon take place” and "the time is near" do not mean everything has already been completed, nor do they mean nothing must happen first. It means that the sovereign process is fully underway, moving toward a certain end on God’s timetable, not ours.

It is worth noticing that early Christians read these passages with this exact, watchful posture. The *Didache*, an early church manual from the late first or early second century, explicitly instructs believers to watch over their lives because they do not know the hour of the Lord’s coming. It openly expects a final apostasy, the appearance of a world deceiver, and the Lord’s return in glory.[^4] Justin Martyr, writing in the middle of the second century, stated that he and many other orthodox believers expected a literal thousand-year reign of Christ on earth in a restored Jerusalem, though he honestly acknowledged that not every faithful Christian agreed.[^5] Irenaeus taught that a final opponent of God would desolate the earth before Christ’s visible return, a framework closely mirrored by Hippolytus.[^6] This expectation was not a novelty invented in recent centuries. Watchful nearness, a final rebellion, Christ’s physical return, and his earthly kingdom formed the bedrock of Christian hope very early in the church.[^7]

One more passage often enters this discussion. Peter writes to early believers who wondered why the Lord had not yet returned: “with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day” (2 Peter 3:8). Peter is not redefining what is near, and we should not use this verse as an excuse to trivialize the delay. His point comes in the very next verse: “The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you” (2 Peter 3:9). What looks like an agonizing delay to us is actually the mercy of God. It is patience with a profound purpose: that people may reach repentance. God is never late, and he is never bound to our calendars.

In summary, John’s use of the words "soon" and "near" invites us to view history through two distinct perspectives. Far from being a simple calendar date or a failed prediction, these opening markers reveal a sophisticated link to ancient prophecy while offering deep reassurance that a divine plan is actively unfolding. It serves as a compelling reminder that the history revealed in Scripture is not a series of random, chaotic events, but a purposeful narrative moving toward the definitive climax recorded in Revelation. Consequently, because the last days are already here, the events of Revelation must be understood throughout history as always being near. This reality calls Christians to a posture of constant readiness, knowing that when these events do unfold, they will be carried out suddenly and quickly.

Verse 2 tells us exactly who John is and what he is doing in this book. He is a witness. He is not passing along secondhand rumors or offering clever human speculation. He is testifying to what he physically saw. The early church identified this author as the apostle John, one of the original Twelve. Justin Martyr said so plainly, and Irenaeus likewise placed the reception of this vision near the end of the Roman Emperor Domitian’s reign.

In verse 9, we get another clear hint that this again is the apostle John speaking to us, rather than a detached outsider. He tells us that he was on the island of Patmos “on account of the word of God and the testimony of Jesus.” He addresses his readers as “your brother and partner in the tribulation and the kingdom and the patient endurance that are in Jesus.” By this point in his life, John is writing as a seasoned, deeply devoted pastor. He is communicating this profound vision out of a profound love and care for the people he knows so well.

This is the first of seven explicit blessings, or beatitudes, found in the book of Revelation, and it meets us right at the front door. Notice the community pattern: one person reads aloud, and the others listen together. Revelation was originally written to be read when the local church gathered for worship, just as Paul expected his letters to be shared corporately among the congregations. The primary way to receive this book, then, is together—with our ears as much as with our eyes.

But reading and hearing are only the beginning. The promised blessing belongs strictly to those who “keep what is written in it.” To keep means to guard, cherish, and obey. Revelation is not merely a complicated puzzle to solve; it is a living word to hold onto. The gritty faithfulness that Christ asks of the seven historical churches in chapters 2 and 3 depends entirely on this posture.

Finally, notice the urgent reason given: “for the time is near.” Centuries earlier, Daniel was told to “shut up the words and seal the book, until the time of the end” (Daniel 12:4). John is told the exact opposite at the close of this book: “Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book, for the time is near” (Revelation 22:10). The book remains wide open for us because the time is near, and Christ himself repeats the comforting promise: “I am coming soon” (Revelation 22:7, 12, 20).

> And he said to me, “These words are trustworthy and true. And the Lord, the God of the spirits of the prophets, has sent his angel to show his servants what must soon take place.” “And behold, I am coming soon. Blessed is the one who keeps the words of the prophecy of this book.” 
> (Revelation 22:6-7)

> “Behold, I am coming soon, bringing my recompense with me, to repay each one for what he has done. I am the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end.” (Revelation 22:12-13)

> He who testifies to these things says, “Surely I am coming soon.” Amen. Come, Lord Jesus! (Revelation 22:20)

### Putting the Scriptures into Practice

Reading the Bible requires the active help of the Holy Spirit. Ask God to guide your reading of Revelation. Pray that he will not only grant intellectual understanding, but show you how to use what you learn to encourage others. I highly suggest reading the book aloud in large sections several times before focusing on individual details. Whenever possible, do this in community with friends and family! In fact, a complete reading of Revelation takes just over an hour.

Also consider journaling your reflections and spending time praying through them. Let the urgency of message to shape your requests in two distinct ways:

1. **Praying for Christ's Return:** The certain promise of Christ's return should inspire an expectant hope within us. Ask God  to help you study Revelation in a manner that moves you to actively pray for the final restoration of all things and the visible return of our King.
2. **Praying for the Persecuted Church:** The extreme pressures faced by the first-century churches, including intense persecution, systemic hardship, and the literal threat of death, are not relics of the past. They remain a dangerous daily reality for millions of believers around the globe today. This call to spiritual alertness should stir a deep commitment in us to intercede regularly for our global brothers and sisters who are standing firm in the face of ongoing trials.    #### Reflection and Discussion Questions

1. **Hearing in Community:** Revelation was written to be read aloud in a corporate gathering. How does changing your approach from a private reading to a shared reading affect the way you absorb its message? What are the practical challenges to reading Scripture "in community" in our modern context, and how can we overcome them?
2. **Holding Fast:** Ask yourself what you can apply from your reading: What do you think the Holy Spirit is telling you to hold onto today? Based on the absolute certainty of God’s sovereign timetable, what specific promises or truths do you need to actively guard and cherish in your current circumstances?
3. **Remaining Faithful:** Where is Christ asking you to remain faithful right now? Are there areas in your work, family, or personal life where the pressure to quiet your witness or match the habits of the culture feels most intense?
4. **Divided Allegiance:** Where are you tempted to give your ultimate trust, worship, or allegiance to something else? In what ways do modern subtle distractions—such as economic safety, career progress, or political identities and leaders—quietly pull our security away from the absolute sovereignty of God?
5. **Wakeful Posture:** If the biblical markers "soon" and "near" represent divine certainty and imminent velocity rather than a human calendar countdown, how should that framework alter your daily priorities? What is the practical difference between living in a state of speculative prediction versus a state of wakeful, sober expectancy?

## Definitions

**Amillennialism (Symbolic-Only View)**: The perspective that interprets the descriptions in Revelation as symbolic spiritual realities rather than a literal future calendar of events. Adherents reject a literal future thousand-year earthly kingdom, focusing instead on Christ's current heavenly rule or His spiritual presence within the Church during the present age between His first and second comings.

**Classic Earthly Kingdom View**: *See Historical Premillennialism.*

**Dispensationalism (Multi-Stage Timeline Systems)**: A systematic theological framework that views the historical unfolding of God's plan through distinct, separate periods or administrations called economies or dispensations. A defining characteristic is the maintenance of a strict structural separation between God's plans for national Israel and His plans for the New Testament Church.

**Future-Event Approach**: *See Historic Futurism.*

**Historic Futurism (Future-Event Approach)**: An approach to biblical interpretation holding that the major prophetic visions and judgments in the book of Revelation describe literal historical occurrences that will take place at the end of the age rather than being entirely fulfilled in antiquity.

**Historical Premillennialism (Classic Earthly Kingdom View)**: The traditional view held widely by the earliest leaders of the church, asserting that Jesus Christ will return physically to the earth after a final period of intense testing (the final trial). Following this visible, post-tribulational return, Christ will establish a literal thousand-year reign of peace on earth, during which the Church remains active in the world as a visible witness.

**Millennial Kingdom (The Thousand-Year Reign)**: The literal thousand-year earthly reign of Jesus Christ following His second coming, described throughout Scripture as a future era characterized by a perfect, righteous government, the absolute subjection of demonic forces, global peace, and spiritual restoration.

**Post-millennialism (Progressive Spiritual Triumph)**: The viewpoint holding that the second coming of Christ will occur after a long era of widespread peace and global spiritual renewal, which is progressively realized in the present age through the triumphant spiritual influence of the Church sharing the gospel.

**Preterism (Ancient-Past Fulfillment)**: An interpretive approach to biblical prophecy holding that most or all of the predictions in the book of Revelation were already entirely fulfilled in the past, primarily during the early centuries of the Christian era.

- **Partial Preterism**: The view that the vast majority of Revelation (chapters 1 through 19) was fulfilled in the ancient past, culminating specifically in the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple in A.D. 70, while still anticipating a future physical resurrection and the personal return of Christ at the very end of history.
- **Full Preterism (Hyper-Preterism)**: The radical view that all biblical prophecy, including the second coming of Christ, the resurrection of the dead, and the creation of the New Heavens and New Earth, was entirely fulfilled in the spiritual realm during the destruction of Jerusalem in A.D. 70.

**Pretribulational Dispensationalism**: A specific variation of dispensationalism holding that Jesus Christ will secretly return to catch up (rapture) the Church into heaven prior to the beginning of a final seven-year period of world history. This framework separates the rapture from the visible second coming of Christ by a seven-year tribulation period.

**The Final Trial (The Great Tribulation)**: An intense, future period of unprecedented global distress, widespread spiritual conflict, severe persecution of the saints, and catastrophic divine judgments that immediately precedes the glorious, visible second coming of Jesus Christ to the earth.
